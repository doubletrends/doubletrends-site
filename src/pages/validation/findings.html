Reading a figure

Every figure has two panels. Left: for each move size (vertical) and number of days ahead (horizontal), how much more often (red) or less often (blue) the market reached that level while the condition held, compared with all days. The circled cells mark the largest swing between a rise and a fall of the same size. Right: the condition’s score (blue line) among the scores of 1,000 synthetic markets (gray bars). To clear, it must sit to the right of the dashed line, the synthetic 95th percentile.

The header of each figure states the range of the condition, its score, the 95th percentile it had to beat, and its p-value: the share of synthetic markets that scored at least as high.

1The golden cross

Traders watch for the 50-day average to cross above the 200-day. Once it runs about 10% above, the left panel looks decisive: the odds of a 4% drop rise, the odds of a 4% rise fall — a 24.5-point swing. Synthetic markets with no memory produce one at least this strong 8.4% of the time.

2Above the 200-day average

“Stay long while price is above the 200-day” is trend following’s first rule. With price 4% to 6% above it, the picture is a near-perfect mirror: rises more likely, drops much less likely — a 27.4-point swing, the largest of the two moving-average conditions. Yet its score sits well inside the synthetic range: 23% of signal-free markets match it.

A large swing and a low score are not a contradiction. The score weighs the whole grid of moves and horizons, and gives more weight to larger moves; a swing concentrated in a few cells can be big and still ordinary. The method defines the score.

3Low bond yields

Cheap money lifts stocks. With the 10-year Treasury yield below 1.34%, the NASDAQ was far more likely to rally 8% within a month: a 42-point swing that grows steadily with time. It is the closest of the four to clearing, and still falls short: 7.6% of synthetic markets match it.

The yield is outside data, so it is held fixed on every synthetic market; only the NASDAQ’s own path is randomized. The test therefore asks whether these particular dates were followed by unusual moves more often than chance would place them.

4Moderate yields

Move the yield to 2.5%–2.9% and the story flips: an 8% drop becomes likelier than a rise — a 21.2-point swing. Read alone, it is a second, bearish regime. It is the weakest of the four: 41% of synthetic markets match it.
